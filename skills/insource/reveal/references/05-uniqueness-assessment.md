# Uniqueness assessment - eight lenses, and the two confusions they prevent

Similarity says how close each competitor is. Uniqueness answers the user's actual question from the other
side: *what here does not already exist, and does that matter?* It is assessed through eight independent
lenses - deliberately not one number, because "is it unique?" has different answers per lens and collapsing
them hides the interesting ones.

## The eight lenses

| Lens | Question | Where the answer comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Conceptual** | How original is the core idea itself? | the idea-axis scores across all competitors |
| **Functional** | How many of the proposed features already exist somewhere? | the feature matrix - which rows are new, which are recombination |
| **Market** | Is the idea already established *in the target market*? | the market dimension filtered to the user's segments |
| **Geographical** | Does it address countries/regions underserved by existing players? | the market + localization dimensions mapped by country |
| **Audience** | Does it serve a neglected group? | the audience axis vs who competitors actually target |
| **Business-model** | Does it introduce a different commercial approach? | the business-model dimension across the set |
| **Implementation** | Does it combine existing capabilities in a meaningfully new way? | the technology + workflow axes |
| **Value-proposition** | Does it deliver something competitors do not adequately deliver? | positioning + user complaints + the gap analysis |

Each lens gets a plain verdict - *genuinely open / partially open / crowded* - with the evidence that put it
there. A lens with no competitor found still says *how hard it looked*, so "open" is never a synonym for
"didn't search."

## The two confusions the lenses exist to prevent

These are the two ways a smart-looking uniqueness section lies. Both must be refused explicitly in the
verdict.

1. **An existing idea is not a lost opportunity.** A project can face many competitors and still be an
   excellent opportunity - if it serves an underserved market or language, if it fixes the thing every
   incumbent's users complain about, or if it combines capabilities the incumbents keep separate. Crowded
   markets with a visible gap beat empty markets with no demand. *High functional similarity + a real
   geographical/audience/value gap is a legitimate "yes, and here's the wedge."*
2. **No competitors is not a green light.** An idea with nobody building it may be genuinely novel - or it may
   be a solution to a problem nobody pays to solve, a market too small, or a category that has died before
   (check the graveyard: defunct similar attempts are the strongest signal about viability). "I found no one
   doing this" must be followed by "and here is what that could mean, including that it could mean nobody
   wants it." *Absence of competition is a finding to interrogate, not a conclusion to celebrate.*

## What the uniqueness section must conclude

Not a single "unique score." It concludes, per lens, where the idea stands, and then synthesizes the two
truths the user actually needs together:

- **What already exists** - the honest prior art, famous and obscure, alive and dead.
- **What is genuinely open** - the specific geography, audience, model, workflow, or value the incumbents
  leave unclaimed, *with the evidence that it is unclaimed and not merely unfilled by the ones you found*.
- **What that means** - the differentiation that is real (visible in competitors' complaints and gaps) versus
  the differentiation the user *hopes* for but the evidence does not support yet.

The synthesis is where Reveal proves it is an investigation and not a cheerleader: it names the openings that
are real, and it says plainly when the "unique angle" is already served, or already tried and abandoned.
