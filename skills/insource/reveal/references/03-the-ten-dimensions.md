# Phase 3 - the ten dimensions every competitor is investigated across

Discovery produced names; investigation produces understanding. For each candidate, fill the ten dimensions
below - each finding goes into the evidence ledger with its source URL and date, labeled fact / estimate /
assumption ([`06`](06-research-quality-and-evidence.md)). A competitor investigated only on its homepage is
investigated not at all: the dimensions that reveal the real threat (traction, complaints, market dominance,
viability) live in stores, reviews, communities, funding records, and changelogs.

## A. Name and identity

- Same name? Similar name? Alternative spellings, transliterations, translations of the name?
- Projects with a *different* name describing the *same* concept (the interesting direction - name similarity
  is a naming problem, concept similarity is a competition problem).
- Are the relevant domains and social handles already taken - and by whom?
- **The trap:** naming similarity is not product similarity. Score them separately; a name collision goes in
  the legal/branding section of the report, never into the similarity score.

## B. Features and functionality

- Which features are identical, partially similar, different, or missing from the competitor?
- Same *workflows*, or same features arranged to do different jobs?
- Which features make the proposed project meaningfully different - and are those differences real or
  cosmetic?
- Build the feature comparison matrix (the proposed project's enumerated features as rows, competitors as
  columns) - it is a report section, not a vibe.
- **The trap:** different vocabulary for the same capability. Two products both "save your place" - one calls
  it a watchlist, one calls it bookmarks. Match on what the feature *does*, not the label.

## C. Market and geography

- Where founded; where headquartered; which countries served; which are the *primary* markets; where the
  revenue actually comes from, if knowable.
- Regional dominance? Local competitors? International footprint or single-country focus? Market segments
  targeted.
- **The trap:** three different facts dressed as one. *Company location* ≠ *service availability* ≠ *market
  dominance*. A US company can be available everywhere and dominant nowhere; a local company can be
  unavailable abroad and dominant at home. Report each separately - the gap analysis depends on distinguishing
  them.

## D. Language and localization

- Which languages supported; is the UI translated or just the marketing?
- RTL support (Arabic/Hebrew/Persian/Urdu); local currencies; country-specific payment methods (e.g. Meeza,
  STC Pay, UPI, iDEAL - the rails that decide whether a global product actually reaches a local market).
- Local regulatory compliance; country-specific features; countries explicitly excluded.
- **The payoff:** this dimension is where underserved markets are *found*, not assumed. A global leader
  without Arabic UI and without local payment rails is not the competitor for an Arabic-first local idea -
  the gap is the finding.

## E. Establishment and history

- When founded vs when the product actually launched (different dates, often years apart); how long it has
  operated.
- Business-model changes; pivots away from the original concept; acquisitions made or taken; whether it is
  actively maintained, stagnant, or dead.
- **The trap:** founding date is not launch date, and a 2019 founding with a 2023 relaunch after a pivot is a
  different competitor than its timeline suggests. Check changelogs, version histories, and Wayback captures
  against the "About" page.

## F. User base and popularity

- Registered users, MAU, DAU, paying customers, website traffic, app downloads, GitHub stars/contributors,
  social followers, community size, growth trend - whatever is *actually* available.
- Use published figures when they exist, with the source; when they do not, estimate from public indicators
  (store download brackets, review counts, traffic estimates, GitHub activity, community member counts) and
  **state the estimation method next to the number**.
- **The trap, stated as the rule it is:** downloads ≠ installs ≠ active users ≠ paying customers; visits ≠
  users; stars ≠ adoption; followers ≠ demand. Never present any proxy as equivalent to active usage. The
  honest phrasing is "estimated tens of thousands of MAU, inferred from 100k+ downloads and a typical
  retention funnel," not "100,000 users."

## G. Business model

- Subscription, advertising, freemium, commission/marketplace take, one-time purchase, enterprise licensing,
  open-source (and its commercial layer: support, hosting, dual license), or genuinely free/community.
- Actual pricing tiers; monetization mechanics; where the model gives it (or denies it) a competitive
  advantage.
- Cross-check the pricing page against the store listing - in-app-purchase prices and web prices often differ.

## H. Technology and implementation (public information only)

- Open or closed source; public repositories and their activity; supported platforms; public APIs and
  integrations; documented architecture; extensibility and customization.
- **The trap:** do not invent internals. If the architecture is not publicly documented, the cell reads "not
  public" - an inferred tech stack from BuiltWith-style signals is an *estimate, method stated*, never a fact.

## I. Positioning and differentiation (what users believe, not what marketing says)

- The problem the competitor *claims* to solve; the audience it targets; the unique advantage it advertises.
- What customers actually say: reviews (store, G2, Capterra, Trustpilot), community complaints, the frequently
  requested-but-missing features, the reasons users leave.
- **The payoff:** the gap between advertised positioning and user complaints is where differentiation
  opportunities live. A product praised for breadth and cursed for complexity is a target an "easy" competitor
  can hit.

## J. Market status and sustainability

- Actively operating? Growing, flat, declining? Release cadence (changelog/store updates are the cheapest
  truth). Funding raised; profitability if public; layoffs, downgrades, service terminations, user exodus
  signals, competitive pressure.
- **The trap:** a company that "exists" and a company that is *winning* are different facts. A funded-but-
  stagnant product and an unfunded-but-growing indie one carry opposite weight in the verdict. Distinguish
  publicly confirmed status from inferred conclusions - a shut-down blog and an unanswered support address are
  *inferred* decline, labeled as such.

## Coverage rule

Every competitor gets all ten dimensions filled with a value, a "not public", or a "not found - searched X" -
never a silent blank. The deep dives in the final report concentrate on the high-similarity competitors, but
the ledger holds all ten for every candidate that survived classification.
