# Structure and length

With content selected and compression method chosen, two decisions remain: order and length. Both are calibrated to the reader, not to the source's own shape or length.

## Bottom-line-first, always

Lead with the conclusion, finding, or recommendation — never with context that delays it (the Pyramid Principle / BLUF, "bottom line up front").

> Weak: "This report examines three approaches to reducing onboarding time... before concluding that a phased rollout of approach two is preferable."
> Strong: "The report recommends a phased rollout of approach two: it cuts onboarding time nearly as much as the aggressive option, at much lower risk."

Reorder even when the source itself builds up to its conclusion — **lead with the destination even when the source led with the journey.** Exception: narrative/chronological sources where sequence is the point may keep supporting detail in order, while the overall takeaway still leads.

## Three layers (Normal mode)

1. **The point** — one or two sentences: conclusion, finding, or recommendation.
2. **Key reasons** — two to four things that most support the point, still compressed.
3. **Supporting detail** — numbers, examples, named sources; included only at longer lengths.

A TL;DR is layer one. An executive summary is layers one and two. A digest adds a thin layer three.

## Extreme mode: flat, not layered

Extreme mode drops the layered-prose structure entirely in favor of a flat list of term + one-line description entries (see templates/extreme-digest.md). The "bottom line first" rule still applies — the first entry is the conclusion/decision, not a supporting detail — but nothing beneath it expands into paragraph form.

## Calibrate to the reader

Before drafting, answer: *who is reading this, and what will they do with it?*

- Deciding whether to engage further → one-liner or TL;DR.
- Will act on the summary alone → executive summary or digest.
- Verifying or building on the work → longer digest, more supporting detail.

If unclear, default to the executive-summary register in Normal mode, state the assumption, and offer to adjust.

## Length as a decision, not a residue

Decide the target length before drafting, not after writing at full length and trimming — trimming after the fact tends to cut unevenly. Rough proportional guide when no explicit length is requested:

| Form | Length | Mode |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | One sentence | Normal |
| TL;DR | 1–3 sentences, ~5% of source | Normal |
| Executive summary | 1 paragraph–1 page, ~10% of source | Normal |
| Digest | Several paragraphs, ~15–25% of source | Normal |
| Detailed recap | Up to ~40% of source | Normal |
| Extreme digest | Shortest possible list of terms + one-liners, no target % — driven by number of distinct must-keep points, not source length | Extreme |

Beyond ~40% in Normal mode, reconsider whether the task is really an edit of the original rather than a summary (see process-business-writing skill).

## Self-contained by default

- Resolve pronouns and references ("this confirms the earlier finding" needs the finding restated).
- Define acronyms on first use.
- Don't refer to the source's own structure ("as discussed in section 3") unless producing an index.
- Give just enough setup that the conclusion makes sense alone.

## Ordering supporting points

Order by importance to the reader, not by order of appearance in the source. Either most-important-first (for readers who may stop anytime) or smallest-to-biggest (for readers committed to reading the whole thing) works; defaulting to the source's own paragraph order does not.

## Quality checks

- [ ] States the conclusion in the first sentence or two.
- [ ] Depth/mode was decided before drafting, matched to the reader.
- [ ] Reads as self-contained: no unresolved pronouns, undefined acronyms, or "as noted above."
- [ ] Supporting points ordered by importance, not source order.
- [ ] Length is deliberate, not a residue of arbitrary cutting.
