# Reading for the point

You cannot summarize what you have not fully understood. This step is invisible in the final output but determines almost all of its quality — rushing it produces the "table of contents" summary that describes a source's shape without saying what it means.

## Read the whole thing before writing anything

Do not summarize as you go, paragraph by paragraph. A point made early is sometimes overturned later; an early example sometimes turns out to be the central case, not a throwaway. For long sources, a first pass for orientation (what kind of piece is this, what does it argue, how is it organized) followed by a second pass for detail beats extracting-while-reading.

## Topic vs. point

Every source has a topic and a point, and they are not the same thing.

> Topic: "This report is about customer churn in Q3."
> Point: "Churn rose 4 points in Q3, driven by a pricing change — not, as the team assumed, by the August outage."

A summary that only captures the topic is a table of contents; a reader who wanted the topic could read the title. Ask explicitly: *if this source could only make one claim, what would it be?* If descriptive rather than argumentative, ask instead: *what does the reader most need to know or do as a result of this existing?*

## Identify the source's own shape

- **Argumentative** — a claim defended by evidence. The point is the claim; lead with it.
- **Informational/reporting** — a finding, with method and context. The point is usually the finding.
- **Narrative/chronological** — events in sequence where order carries meaning. The point may be an outcome or turning point; identify it even though the source doesn't lead with it.
- **Comparative** — options assessed against criteria. The point is usually the recommendation or key differentiator.
- **Reference/procedural** — meant to be consulted, not read start to finish. Here "the point" is closer to "what the reader needs to find quickly."

Misjudging this causes real errors: summarizing an argumentative piece chronologically when the reader needs the conclusion first, or flattening a narrative into a single claim when sequence was the point.

## Locate the load-bearing parts

Mark the sentences the rest of the piece depends on. Test: *if this sentence were deleted, would something later stop making sense, or would the argument have a hole?* If yes, it's load-bearing — a must-keep candidate (see 02-selection.md). Load-bearing is not the same as *frequently repeated*; a source can restate its main point five times and mention its key caveat once.

Load-bearing material usually includes: the stated conclusion; the one or two pieces of evidence it most depends on; any explicit caveat, limitation, or dissent; definitions a reader needs before later points make sense.

## Notice what the source is uncertain about

Separate what the source states as settled from what it hedges, disputes, or flags as provisional — this distinction is lost easily during compression (see 05-faithfulness-check.md), so notice it now. Watch for: "may," "suggests," "preliminary," "we believe," explicit disagreement between sources, and any place the source flags its own assumption.

## Before drafting, be able to state

- The source's **type** (argumentative, informational, narrative, comparative, reference).
- Its **one-sentence point**.
- The **two or three load-bearing pieces** the point depends on.
- Any **material caveat or uncertainty** the source itself flags.

If you can't produce these four, the reading pass isn't done.
